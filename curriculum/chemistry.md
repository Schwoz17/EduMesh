# Chemistry

## Atomic Structure
Level: SS1

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. It has a nucleus at the centre containing protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge). Electrons (negative charge) move around the nucleus in shells or energy levels.

The atomic number is the number of protons. The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons. Sodium has atomic number 11 and mass number 23, so it has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 are isotopes of chlorine.

Electrons fill shells in order: the first shell holds up to 2, the second up to 8, and the third up to 8 for the first 20 elements. Sodium's electronic configuration is 2, 8, 1.

## Chemical Bonding
Level: SS1–SS2

Atoms bond to achieve a stable outer shell, usually 8 electrons (the octet rule).

Ionic (electrovalent) bonding happens when a metal gives electrons to a non-metal. Sodium gives one electron to chlorine to form Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, which attract to form sodium chloride, common table salt. Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water.

Covalent bonding happens when non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons. In a water molecule, oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms. Covalent compounds usually have low melting points and do not conduct electricity.

Metallic bonding holds metal atoms together in a sea of free electrons, which is why metals like copper wire conduct electricity.

## Acids, Bases and Salts
Level: SS1–SS2

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water. Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red. Examples include the citric acid in lemon and orange, and the ethanoic acid in vinegar.

A base is a substance that reacts with an acid to form salt and water. A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali. Alkalis feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. Examples include sodium hydroxide used in local soap making and the potash (kaun) used in cooking.

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. A pH below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral (pure water) and above 7 is alkaline.

Neutralisation is the reaction of an acid with a base to give salt and water: acid + base → salt + water. Farmers add lime to acidic soil to neutralise it, and people take antacid for stomach acid.

## Separation Techniques
Level: JSS3–SS1

Mixtures can be separated by physical methods because their parts are not chemically joined.

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, like removing sand from water. Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid, like getting salt from sea water. Distillation separates a liquid from a solution by boiling and condensing the vapour, used to purify water.

Decantation means pouring off a liquid and leaving the settled solid, like pouring water off washed rice. Winnowing uses wind to separate chaff from grains such as millet and guinea corn. Sieving separates particles of different sizes, like sieving garri.

Chromatography separates the dyes in ink, and a separating funnel separates immiscible liquids such as palm oil and water.

## Rates of Chemical Reaction
Level: SS2

The rate of a reaction is how fast reactants are used up or products are formed.

Factors that increase the rate: higher temperature makes particles move faster and collide more often; higher concentration puts more particles in the same space; smaller particle size (larger surface area) exposes more particles, which is why powdered firewood ignites faster than logs; and a catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up.

Enzymes are biological catalysts. Yeast enzymes speed up the fermentation used in making burukutu and bread.

Refrigeration slows down the reactions that spoil food, which is why fish lasts longer in a freezer.
