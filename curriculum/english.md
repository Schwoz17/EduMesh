# English

## Parts of Speech
Level: JSS1–SS1

Every English word belongs to a part of speech, which tells us the job the word does in a sentence. There are eight parts of speech.

A noun names a person, place, thing or idea, for example Chiamaka, Ibadan, bicycle and honesty. A pronoun replaces a noun, for example she, they and it. A verb shows an action or a state, for example run, sell and is. An adjective describes a noun, for example a busy market or a red bucket. An adverb describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb, and often ends in -ly, for example quickly or carefully.

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun and another word, for example in, on, under and beside: the keke is beside the gate. A conjunction joins words or clauses, for example and, but, because and although. An interjection expresses sudden feeling, for example Ah!, Wow! or Ehen!

To find the part of speech of a word, look at how it is used in that sentence. In "She will fish tomorrow", fish is a verb, but in "She bought fish", fish is a noun.

## Concord (Subject-Verb Agreement)
Level: SS1–SS3

Concord means agreement between the subject and the verb of a sentence. A singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb. For example: The boy plays football. The boys play football.

Rules that WAEC often tests. Two subjects joined by "and" take a plural verb: Tunde and Musa are classmates. With "either…or" and "neither…nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject: Neither the teacher nor the students were late.

Words such as everyone, everybody, each, nobody and anyone are singular: Everyone is present. A collective noun such as committee, team or family takes a singular verb when the group acts as one unit: The committee has made its decision.

"One of" takes a singular verb because the subject is one: One of the girls is my sister. Words joined to the subject by "as well as" or "together with" do not change the verb: The principal, as well as the teachers, is in the hall.

## Summary Writing
Level: SS2–SS3

Summary writing tests your ability to pick out the main points of a passage and express them briefly in your own words. It is a compulsory part of the WAEC English paper.

Steps: First, read the whole passage carefully to understand the general idea. Second, read the question to know exactly what points are required, for example "the advantages of solar power". Third, identify the topic sentence of each relevant paragraph, which usually carries the main point. Fourth, write each point as one short, complete sentence.

Avoid examples, illustrations, repetitions and figures of speech, because they are not main points. Do not copy whole sentences from the passage; lifting is penalised. Write only the number of points asked for, numbered if the question requests it.

Example: From a passage about markets, a good summary point is "Markets provide jobs for many people", not a long description of the traders at Oshodi.

## Letter Writing
Level: JSS3–SS3

There are two main kinds of letters: formal and informal. A semi-formal letter sits between them.

An informal letter is written to a friend or relative. It has the writer's address and date at the top right, a friendly salutation such as "Dear Amina,", a body written in a relaxed style, and a closing such as "Your friend," followed by the first name only.

A formal letter is written to an official such as a principal, a chairman of a local government or the editor of a newspaper. It has two addresses: the writer's address at the top right and the receiver's address on the left. It has a formal salutation such as "Dear Sir," and a title in capital letters that states the purpose of the letter, for example "REQUEST FOR A NEW BOREHOLE IN OUR COMMUNITY". The body is serious and direct, without slang or contractions, and the closing is "Yours faithfully," followed by the full name and signature.

A semi-formal letter is written to someone you respect but know, such as an uncle or a teacher. It ends with "Yours sincerely,".

## Figures of Speech
Level: SS1–SS3

Figures of speech are expressions that use words in a special way to create an effect.

A simile compares two things using "like" or "as": She is as busy as a bee. A metaphor compares two things directly without "like" or "as": My father is a lion at home. Personification gives human qualities to a non-living thing: The wind whispered through the palm trees.

Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration: I have told you a million times. Euphemism is a mild way of saying something unpleasant: He passed away instead of he died. Irony says the opposite of what is meant or expected: A fire station burning down.

An oxymoron puts two opposite words together: an open secret. Alliteration repeats the same first sound in nearby words: Peter's pepper pot. A proverb is a short traditional saying, common in Nigerian speech: When the drumbeat changes, the dance must change.
